How to Promote an Event: 9 Channels That Actually Work (with 2026 Numbers)

How to sell out your event without wasting money: email, organic social, paid ads, partners, local PR. Average costs, realistic conversion rates, and the optimal sequence.

In short: nine channels genuinely work for promoting an event, but not all at once and not for every event type. The classic winning sequence is: your own list + partners, then organic social, then paid ads and local PR. The key is to measure every channel with UTMs and calculate the CAC (cost of acquisition) per source. Without measurement, event marketing is blind: you know how many tickets you sold, but not why — and you can’t replicate the result.

The timing of your promotion

Before you switch on any channel, you need to understand when and in what order to use it. The rule of thumb that works best in practice is the 60-40-20:

  • 60% of your audience should be reached in the early phase, more than four weeks before the event. These are your early adopters, the most motivated: low acquisition cost, no need to manufacture urgency.
  • 40% mid-funnel, between two and four weeks out: a broader audience that needs more nudges (retargeting, reminders, social proof from people who have already signed up).
  • 20% last-call, the final 48 hours: lean on urgency and scarcity. This phase pays for itself with a conversion spike, but it doesn’t replace the first two — it arrives too late for anyone who has already decided to ignore the event.

Timing also depends on ticket price. For the technical setup that lets you take registrations the moment you launch — page live, payments active, automatic confirmation — see the online ticketing guide: it’s best to have everything ready at least three weeks before the public launch.

Event type Recommended lead time
Free event 3-6 weeks
Paid event < €50 6-10 weeks
Paid event > €100 or B2B 12-16 weeks

Channel 1 — Your own email list

This is the channel with the lowest cost per acquisition of all nine. If you already have a list of contacts interested in your topic, every email you send costs essentially nothing and converts better than any ad.

Industry average numbers for events:

Metric Benchmark
Open rate 25-40%
CTR on the registration link 2-5%
Final conversion (click → ticket) 2-8%

A typical sequence for a paid event involves at least three emails:

  1. Save the date (registration opens): a clean announcement, early-bird pricing, a direct link to the page.
  2. Reminder + value (-10 days): one more reason to come — a speaker, the agenda, a testimonial from a past attendee.
  3. Last call (-48 hours): real urgency (remaining seats, early-bird about to expire, or a price increase).

For segmentation, separate your engaged contacts (opened at least 2 emails in the last 90 days) from the cold ones. Email the engaged first, with direct subject lines. For the cold segment, use curiosity-gap subject lines and test on a small sample first.

Subject lines that work: “[Event name] — early-bird seats are running out” beats “Event X — register now” by roughly 30% in open rate, based on tests on a typical list.

Channel 2 — Partners & sponsors

A well-chosen partner can drive 20-40% of total registrations without spending a cent on ads. The mechanism is simple: you have something their audience values (content, a relationship, access), and they have a list that’s valuable to you.

How to pick the right partner:

  • An audience aligned with your target but not in direct competition with your core business
  • An active email list with an open rate above 20% (ask them up front — anyone who doesn’t know or won’t say is a red flag)
  • The ability to do active promotion, not just a link in a newsletter footer

The typical trade is a swap: you promote their content or event in your channels, and they do the same for you. If the value isn’t symmetrical (a much bigger partner), offer free access, a speaking slot, or co-branded visibility.

Tag every partner with separate UTMs (utm_source=partner-companyname&utm_medium=email). If a partner sends you 500 clicks from a qualified audience, that’s worth €500-1,500 of paid traffic — a measurable value you can bring to the table when negotiating the next event.

Channel 3 — Organic social

The most-used channel, but often the least efficient for anyone without an established community. The numbers are clear: organic reach of 5-15% of your followers, and a follower-to-registrant conversion of 0.3-1%.

With 2,000 Instagram followers you can expect 100-300 people exposed per post and 6-20 clicks to the registration page. It’s not your primary acquisition engine, but it keeps your existing audience warm and generates visible social proof.

By platform:

Platform Format that works Target audience
Instagram Reels (30-60s), Stories countdowns Consumer events, under 40
LinkedIn Text post + a question, native video B2B, professionals, HR
TikTok Native Reels, behind-the-scenes content Under 30, cultural events
Facebook Facebook Event, posts in industry groups Local community, over 35

Minimum frequency: one post every 3 days in the 3 weeks before the event. Don’t post only announcements — alternate value content (speaker previews, agenda teasers, audience questions) with direct registration posts.

Channel 4 — Meta Ads (Instagram + Facebook)

The first paid channel to test for consumer events. The advantage of Meta Ads is targeting by interests and behaviors: you can reach people who don’t follow you yet but who are statistically similar to your past attendees.

Average CPM: €5-15 depending on the sector and seasonal competition. With €30/day for 10 days you’ll have enough data to tell whether the channel works for your specific event.

Recommended targeting structure for events:

  1. Custom audience — upload your email list and past attendees. These are the people most likely to convert.
  2. Lookalike 2-5% from the custom audience — Meta finds similar profiles in the wider population.
  3. Interest stack — layer 3-5 interests related to your event (never just one, that’s too broad).

Formats that convert for events:

  • Reel/Video (6-15 seconds): high impressions, low CPM, great for awareness.
  • Carousel with 3-4 cards: speaker, agenda, venue — each card is one more reason to sign up.
  • Stories with a countdown sticker: visual urgency in the final 48 hours.

Always measure the cost per click to the registration page (CPC), not just impressions. A CPC above €2.50 for a consumer event under €50 is a sign of bad targeting or weak copy.

Channel 5 — Google Ads

Google Ads works for events only if there’s a relevant search query with meaningful volume. The question to ask before launching a campaign is: “what does someone who wants to attend an event like mine type into Google?”

When it makes sense to invest:

  • A B2B conference on a specific technical topic (“cybersecurity conference 2026”) — the query exists and intent is high.
  • A festival or consumer event with a recognizable brand (“[Festival name] tickets”) — people search for your name.
  • Competitive categories where you want to appear above your competitors.

When it’s a waste:

  • A generic consumer event (“things to do this Saturday”) — huge volume, very low intent, CPC burns budget without converting.
  • An ultra-niche event with an audience of a few hundred people in the whole country — the query doesn’t exist.

Suggested campaign structure: three separate groups — brand (your event’s name), non-brand (category + city), competitor (people already searching for similar events). Use phrase or exact match types, never broad on an event campaign with a limited budget.

Check search volume with Keyword Planner before investing: if your main query has fewer than 100 searches/month, Google Ads isn’t the right channel for you.

Channel 6 — LinkedIn Ads (B2B only)

LinkedIn Ads is the most expensive channel of all — average CPC €5-15, CPM between €30-80 — but it’s the only one that lets you target by job title, seniority, and industry with surgical precision.

It only makes sense to invest when your average ticket exceeds €300-500 and your typical attendee is a professional or decision-maker who needs to be convinced with rational arguments (ROI, credentials, headline speakers).

Targeting that works for B2B events:

  • Job title + seniority (e.g. “HR Manager”, “Operations Director”, “Senior” and above)
  • A specific industry (not “all industries” — that burns your budget)
  • Company size (e.g. 50-500 employees)

Formats: a Single Image Ad with longer copy and a concrete data point in the first line. The LinkedIn audience reads — it’s not the place for purely visual creativity. A message like “73% of CFOs can’t measure the ROI of training — we can. [Workshop, June 15]” converts better than a glossy banner.

Don’t test LinkedIn Ads with less than €500 of budget: below that threshold you won’t accumulate enough data to optimize.

Channel 7 — Local PR + traditional media

Often underrated, especially for consumer events with a strong local angle. An article in a local newspaper or regional edition is worth more than 10 Instagram posts in terms of perceived credibility and, indirectly, of SEO for your event.

How to increase your pickup rate:

  • Send the press release with 5-7 days of lead time before your desired publication date — local newsrooms have a short agenda but still work with a few days’ notice.
  • Include a local fact or data point that makes the story relevant to that specific outlet’s readers (“the first [category] event in [city] in three years”).
  • Offer a short interview (15 minutes by phone) to the journalist — a person is the narrative hook that local articles are always looking for.

Beyond print: industry blogs and niche podcasts are often willing to run a preview or an interview in exchange for a press pass or reserved seat. Identify the 3-5 with the audience best aligned to your event and reach out 3-4 weeks in advance.

The pickup rate (how many of your pitches turn into an article) is typically 20-40% for well-targeted local PR, and close to zero for generic releases blasted out indiscriminately.

Channel 8 — Early bird + early access

The early bird isn’t just a discount — it’s the main acquisition tool in the first weeks of promotion. People who buy early aren’t doing it to save money (the difference is often just €10-20): they do it because they’re already motivated, and the discount lowers the decision friction at a moment when the event still feels far off.

A typical structure that works:

  • A 20-30% discount off the full price, valid for the first 7 days of sales or the first 50 tickets (whichever comes first).
  • A visible timer on the registration page — not a fake countdown that resets, but a real timer with an expiry date and time.
  • An email reminder when 48 hours of early-bird remain, even if the person hasn’t bought yet.

In practice, the early bird alone unlocks 50-65% of registrations before the mid-funnel — it eases the pressure on the final week and gives you early signals on how well the event is performing.

To design a registration page with the early-bird timer positioned so it actually converts, see the guide on building an event registration page that converts: it has specific benchmarks on where to place the CTA and how to communicate scarcity without feeling contrived.

Channel 9 — Posters and flyers

For consumer events with a strong local geographic angle only. A summer festival in a mid-sized town, a market, a gig at a venue: physical posters reach people who aren’t searching for you online and will never see your Instagram post.

Where it works:

  • Bars and venues that match your demographic: €30-100 per location (printing + an arrangement with the venue), estimated conversion rate of 0.5-2% of foot traffic during the display period.
  • Flyers handed out at a similar prior event: if you attend, as a sponsor or simple visitor, an event with a comparable audience, 500 flyers generate a 3-8% QR scan rate — the contact is already warm.
  • Partner materials (lounges, waiting areas, sports club locker rooms): heavily dependent on audience alignment.

Operational rule for measurement: use QR codes with a different UTM for each location (utm_source=flyer&utm_medium=print&utm_campaign=event-xyz&utm_content=bar-name). Without separate UTMs, you’ll know the flyers “maybe” worked, but you’ll have no proof.

A realistic budget for a local poster campaign (20-30 locations): €200-600 all in. It’s not the channel for big numbers, but it’s excellent for generating qualified local awareness at predictable fixed costs.

Measurement: UTMs, GA4, CAC per channel

All nine channels above become data only with measurement. And without data, every decision about your next event is a guess — often an expensive one.

The minimum UTM structure for every link:

utm_source     → channel (email, instagram, linkedin, partner-companyname, google)
utm_medium     → type (email, social, paid, referral, print)
utm_campaign   → event name (e.g. workshop-hr-june-2026)
utm_content    → variant (e.g. earlybird, reminder, last-call, reel-v1)

Every link that leaves your channels should carry UTMs — including links in social bios, organic posts, and partner emails. A link without UTMs is a ticket sold whose origin you’ll never know.

In GA4, set up a specific conversion event for “ticket purchase completed” (or “registration completed” for free events). GA4 attributes the conversion to the last session with UTMs — not perfect, but good enough for budget decisions.

CAC per channel formula:

Channel CAC = total channel spend / tickets attributed to that channel (via UTM)

A concrete example: if you spent €400 on Meta Ads and got 32 registrations with utm_source=instagram, your Instagram CAC is €12.50. Compare it to the average ticket value (€40): ROAS = 3.2×. Below 4× means that channel is operating at a loss relative to the event’s margin.

Not measuring isn’t a neutral choice — it’s one of the most expensive mistakes in event management, as we describe in our list of mistakes to avoid in event organization.

A sample sequence for a 300-attendee event

Here’s the week-by-week operational sequence for a paid consumer event (€40 ticket) targeting 300 registrations. Starting 12 weeks before the event.

Week Priority action
-12 Page live, early bird active. Save-the-date email to your own list. Partner agreements activated.
-10 Organic social posts 3×/week. First email to partners for a promotion swap.
-8 Launch Meta Ads retargeting page visitors + lookalike of the email list. Budget €30/day.
-6 Check early-bird conversion (target: 40% of tickets sold). If below, accelerate list emails.
-5 Early-bird reminder email “last 7 days”. Local PR activated (press release).
-4 Early bird closes. Switch to full price. “Sold XX tickets” post for social proof.
-3 Raise Meta Ads budget to €50/day. Podcast/industry-blog outreach.
-2 Mid-funnel email with speaker/agenda content. Instagram Reels teasers.
-1 Last-call email + 48-hour timer. Stories countdown. Possible Google Ads brand campaign on the event name.
Day 0 Check remaining capacity. “Few seats left” only if true — fake scarcity burns the list.

The critical checkpoints are at week -6 (are you at 40%?) and week -2 (are you at 80%?). If you’re behind, the fastest lever is almost always your own email list with a fresh incentive, not bumping the ads budget, which needs 3-5 days to optimize.

Conclusion

Promoting an event doesn’t mean running all nine channels at once — it means figuring out which three or four work for your event type, your budget, and your timing, and executing them well.

The sequence that works for most events: start with your own list and partners (zero or near-zero cost), add the early bird to open sales fast, then scale with paid ads only once you already have real conversion data from the page.

Every channel has a different cost, conversion rate, and ramp-up curve — the one unrecoverable mistake is not measuring. Frequently asked questions

How much budget should I allocate to promote an event?
Industry rule of thumb: 10-15% of the event's projected gross revenue for promotion. Example: a 300-ticket event × €40 = €12K target, so a marketing budget of €1,200-1,800. For free events, calculate the budget based on your target cost per lead multiplied by the number of attendees you're aiming for.
How far in advance should I start promoting?
It depends on pricing and event type: free events 3-6 weeks out, with registrations peaking in the final week. Paid events under €50 6-10 weeks out. Paid events over €100 or B2B 12-16 weeks out — B2B decision-makers have long buying cycles and annual budgets. An early-bird tier unlocks 60% of the acquisition work.
Email vs Instagram: which converts better?
Your own email list: 2-8% conversion rate among active contacts. Organic Instagram: 0.3-1% of exposed followers. Well-targeted Instagram ads: 1-3% of clicks. Bottom line: email is the single most profitable channel, but it doesn't scale beyond your list — Instagram ads are there to fill the list BEFORE the event, not to replace it.
Should I run Google Ads for my event?
Yes, only if there's a relevant search query with meaningful volume. Niche technical B2B events ("AI conference 2026") yes. Generic consumer events ("things to do this Saturday") no — volume is high but intent is low and CPCs burn through budget. Measure with Keyword Planner before you invest.
How much is a partner or sponsor worth in promotional terms?
A partner with an aligned audience can drive 20-40% of total registrations if the promotion is well built: a dedicated post, an email to their list, an affiliate link with UTM tracking. Value it as the equivalent CPC of the traffic they bring — if a partner sends you 500 clicks from a qualified audience, that's worth €500-1,500 of paid traffic.
Do QR codes on a physical poster still work?
Yes, if placed in the right spot. Posters in bars/venues in your target area: 0.5-2% conversion rate on foot traffic. Flyers handed out at a similar prior event: 3-8%. QR codes on partner materials (lounges, waiting areas): variable. Key point: use a different UTM for each location to measure what works.
How close to the event should I close ticket sales?
Never close sales before the event if you still have capacity — many organizers lose 10-20% of revenue with cut-offs that are too early. Raise the price in the final 48 hours (last call) instead of closing. Only close if printing physical tickets (badges, materials) requires an early cut-off.
How do I measure the ROI of my promotion?
Three numbers: (1) CAC per channel = channel spend / tickets sold from that channel (requires UTMs on every link), (2) attendee LTV = average revenue per attendee, including likely repeat attendance, (3) ROAS = revenue / marketing spend. A healthy event has ROAS > 4× on its direct marketing spend.
